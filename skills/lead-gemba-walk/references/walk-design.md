## Walk types

| Type | Who walks | What they do | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Value-stream walk | All managers who touch the value stream, led by its owner | Follow the product end to end, across departments; count steps, time and information flows; ask how the same value could be made with far fewer steps | Womack |
| Management-system walk | A leader, often with the leaders of the area | Watch how the daily management system works: boards, trade-offs, reactions to reds. Observe; fix nothing | Womack |
| Problem walk (ad hoc) | Senior leaders or staff | Go to the front line to get to the bottom of one problem. Womack questions its value where a sound daily management system handles problems | Womack |
| Process walk | The area leader or a manager, with the team | Observe the work against its standard, find waste and obstacles. A common practitioner form; Womack's types do not name it | KaiNexus; Shook |

In one plant example the managers had been to the gemba before a morning meeting, and the senior team then walked board to board looking only at the reds (Womack). A management-system walk can follow that pattern: board first, then the source of one red.

## Three things to assess

Assess every gemba through purpose, process and people (Shook):

- **Purpose:** what is the area trying to achieve, and is management aligning people and processes to it?
- **Process:** is the work designed to achieve the purpose, and done that way?
- **People:** are the people engaged and supported by the process?

Shook usually starts with a few questions on purpose and problems, spends most of the walk on process, and returns to purpose at the end.

## Question order and question bank

Order: **what → why → what if → why not**. Grasp the situation before diagnosing it; "ask no why before its time" (Shook, crediting David Verble).

Core questions (Mann, paraphrased):

1. What is the process here?
2. How can you tell it is working?
3. What do you do when it is not working?
4. Is there work going on to improve it?

More open questions (KaiNexus, paraphrased):

- Who supplies your inputs, and who receives your output?
- How do you know what to do next?
- Are the materials and information there when you need them?
- What slows you down or makes you wait?
- How do you handle an exception or a bad part?
- What would you change first?

On boards, ask how the board works for the team, not whether it is up to date. A team that says posted problems have stayed open for months shows a broken link in the system (Mann).

Avoid closed or leading questions such as "this works well, doesn't it?": they signal the expected answer (practitioner consensus, rule of thumb).

## What to look for, by walk type

**Process walk** (KaiNexus; Shook):

- The written standard against the actual work. Do not assume they match.
- Waiting, walking, searching, reaching: motion and waiting waste.
- Work pulled by the next step, or output piling up in front of it.
- Inputs available when needed; materials within reach.
- 5S condition.
- Variation between people or shifts doing the same task.
- Hand-offs between steps.
- Overburden (muri) and unevenness (mura), not only waste (muda) (Shook).
- Workarounds: problems people fix themselves every time.

**Management-system walk** (Womack; Mann):

- Boards show today's actuals against target.
- Each red has a countermeasure or an escalation, with an owner and a date.
- Escalations get an answer back.
- Actions are closed on time; old ones are visible.
- Leader standard work is done and checked by the next level up.
- There is evidence of improvement tied to what the board shows.

**Value-stream walk** (Womack):

- The process steps from receiving to shipping, in order.
- Inventory or work in process between steps (count it).
- Cycle times, changeover times and uptime at each step, as observed or as quoted (label which).
- The information flow: how each step learns what to make next.

Shook's four lenses help here: the **solution** lens (looking for places to apply a tool) is the weakest; the **waste** lens is incomplete until causes are found; prefer the **problem** lens (objectives, gaps, causes) and the **kaizen** lens (patterns and flow, starting near the customer).

## Respect rules

- First rule: do no harm. Do not prescribe from afar; diagnose close to the work, with the people who do it (Shook).
- Respect → rely on → develop → challenge. Challenge is part of respect (Shook).
- No evaluation of individuals, no punitive tone, no hunt for someone to blame (KaiNexus).
- No fixes on the spot; reflect first and change through an improvement cycle with the people who do the work (KaiNexus).

## Cadence

Sources disagree:

| Source | Front-line leaders | Senior leaders |
|---|---|---|
| Murli, LEI | daily, as leader standard work on a fixed route and time | — |
| Womack | management-system walk daily; value-stream walk periodic | problem walks ad hoc |
| KaiNexus FAQ | weekly | monthly |

Use these as a range, not a rule: front-line leaders daily to weekly, area and value-stream managers weekly, plant leaders weekly to monthly (rule of thumb, synthesised from the table). Vary the day, time and shift now and then to see other conditions (KaiNexus). Consistent follow-through matters more than frequency (KaiNexus).

## Gemba walk, LPA and walking around

- Walking around without a theme, prepared questions, restraint and follow-up collects anecdotes; Womack links the ad hoc walk to "management by walking around" and questions it.
- An LPA is a formal, scheduled, checklist-based check by several layers (CQI-8, as summarised by EASE). It asks whether the standard is followed. A gemba walk also asks whether the standard is adequate and what is in the way. Run an LPA as one segment of a walk, not as the whole walk.
- Walks and leader standard work can decay into a compliance, check-the-box activity; the point is the conversation with the front line (Mann).
- Dashboards do not replace presence at the gemba; go-and-see is a step of the shopfloor-management loop (IEOM 2024).
