# Routine audit

How to audit a running tier-meeting routine: the observation checklist, the adherence metrics, and the evidence and failure modes behind them.

## Contents

- Failure modes

## How to observe

- Score real meetings, not management's account of them. In the audits, observers filled in yes/no sheets during real meetings (ZielKom report), and the check-up after an introduction sends neutral observers to real meetings for the same reason (ifaa).
- Observe more than one meeting per tier before you score it (rule of thumb).
- Answer each question Yes or No for what you saw. Write "not observed" when you could not see it, and never score a report as an observation.

## Audit areas and questions

Five audit areas, with the questions paraphrased (ZielKom report).

**1. Performance management**

1. Is each KPI specific and measurable, with a target and a time frame?
2. Is each actual compared with its target on the board?
3. Are deviations recorded when they happen?
4. Does each deviation lead to an action, an escalation or problem solving?

**2. Meeting process (Ablaufmanagement)**

1. Is there a written agenda?
2. Does the meeting follow it, briskly and on purpose?
3. Does the meeting keep to its standard duration?
4. Is the meeting clearly aimed at targets and actions, not at reporting?

**3. Problem solving**

1. Is problem solving started for deviations with an unknown cause?
2. Is each started problem solve tracked to its end?
3. Does it follow a structured approach with standard documents?
4. Are the problem definition and the root cause written down?
5. Are countermeasures carried out as agreed, checked, and then made the standard?

**4. Visual management**

1. Does the board show the current production status?
2. Is the agenda on the board?
3. Is the KPI profile on the board?
4. Is the problem solving in progress visible on the board?

**5. Competence development**

1. Do team members take part in solving problems, not only in reporting them?
2. Do team members help carry out the countermeasures?
3. Is the routine linked to competence management, for example a qualification matrix?

## Adherence metrics

Measure over a fixed period, for example one week as in the self-audit template (set by local policy). Targets are set by local policy.

| Metric | Formula | Worked example (invented round numbers) |
|---|---|---|
| Meetings held % | held ÷ scheduled × 100 | 16 ÷ 20 × 100 = 80% |
| Started on time % | started on time ÷ held × 100 | 12 ÷ 16 × 100 = 75% |
| Within timebox % | ended within the timebox ÷ held × 100 | 8 ÷ 16 × 100 = 50% |
| Attendance % | attendees present ÷ attendees invited × 100, summed over the meetings held | 120 ÷ 160 × 100 = 75% |
| Reds with a countermeasure or escalation % | (reds with countermeasure or escalation) ÷ reds × 100 | 30 ÷ 40 × 100 = 75% |
| Action closure rate % | actions closed by their due date ÷ actions due × 100 | 15 ÷ 20 × 100 = 75% |
| Average open age (days) | sum of the ages of all open actions ÷ number of open actions | (2 + 4 + 6 + 8 + 10) ÷ 5 = 6 days |
| Escalation answer time (hours) | time the answer reached the raising team − time raised, averaged | (2 + 4 + 6) ÷ 3 = 4 hours |

In the examples, 20 meetings were scheduled in one week (4 tiers × 5 days). In the attendance example, 10 people are invited to each of the 16 meetings held, so 160 places were invited and 120 were filled.

## Evidence

- **Adherence is the binding constraint.** A 12-week tiered-huddle pilot at a US medical centre held 68% of the possible huddles (81 of 120), and 99% of the huddles held started and ended on time (Merchant et al. 2022). Measure whether the routine happens before you judge its design.
- **Weak problem solving is the most common gap.** In audits at three German manufacturers, the meeting process and visual management were mostly strong, while problem solving and competence development were weak or missing (ZielKom report). In German expert interviews, teams discussed causes instead of reading out actuals only in rare cases (BVL).
- **Sustained gains need follow-up review.** In 65 kaizen events at 8 manufacturers, post-event performance review and acceptance of the changes predicted whether results held 9–18 months later (Glover et al. 2011).

## Failure modes

| Failure mode | What you see | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Status reporting instead of problem solving | Every KPI is read out; no owner or date leaves the meeting | ZielKom report; BVL |
| Too many KPIs | Boards fill up with automatic KPIs and lose focus | IEOM 2024, citing Meißner et al. 2018 |
| Weak moderation | Low participation; the leader talks and the team listens | ZielKom report |
| Verbal, untracked escalation | Escalations are not written down; actions are copied between board, spreadsheet and notes | BVL |
| Posted problems are never acted on | The team sees the board as wall decoration | Mann, via LeanBlog |
| Fear of red | People report numbers selectively because they fear showing true performance | BVL |
| Leaders retreat to screens | Managers stop going to the shop floor because the data is on a screen | IEOM 2024 |
| Gains fade | Results slide back without follow-up review, or after the leaders who drove them move on | Glover et al. 2011; Murli, LEI |
